[FULLTEXT: Results:] The mean RTP for ruptured Achilles injuries (n = 20) was 229 days, with no significant difference between 2010-2015 and 2016-2021 (P = .718). Nonruptured injuries (n = 352) had a significantly shorter RTP of 14.9 days versus rupture injuries with an RTP of 229 days (P < .001). Player age was significantly associated with RTP time, with younger players (≤26 years) exhibiting prolonged RTP durations (303.56 ± 112.97 days) compared to older players (>26 years) (168.09 ± 99.66 days; P = .017). There was no difference in RTP with field type (artificial turf vs grass, P = .59). Reinjury rates increased over time but were not statistically significant (P = .77). Following multivariate analysis, injury type was the only statistically significant factor affecting RTP duration, with ruptured Achilles injuries associated with significantly increased time missed compared to nonrupture injuries (95% CI, 171.15-264.11 days; P < .001). Forwards with ruptured Achilles injuries exhibited a decline in games played in the index year (−9.00 ± 8.49 vs 3.00 ± 1.73 games; P = .041). Midfielders with ruptured Achilles injuries demonstrated a significantly greater increase in goals than controls in the index year (1.00 ± 1.41 vs −0.43 ± 0.25 goals; P = .004) and a smaller decline in assists than controls (−0.50 ± 2.83 vs −1.00 ± 2.31 assists; P = .024) relative to preinjury performance. Among goalkeepers, rupture injuries were significantly associated with declines in games played in the first year postinjury (−20.50 ± 16.26 vs 1.00 ± 1.26 games; P = .050) and in the second year postinjury (−31.00 ± 4.24 vs −3.50 ± 5.85 games; P = .006). Rupture injuries were associated with a greater decline in clean sheets in the first year postinjury (−6.01 ± 0.71 vs −0.75 ± 2.99 clean sheets; P = .041) and second year postinjury (−5.98 ± 0.02 vs −2.50 ± 2.08 clean sheets; P = .024).
[FULLTEXT: Return to Play Following Ruptured Achilles Injuries] RTP following ruptured Achilles tendon injuries (n = 20) averaged 218.2 ± 97.8 and 239.9 ± 158.6 days for 2010-2015 and 2016-2021, respectively. RTP duration did not differ significantly by year of injury, playing surface, or injury setting (P > .05, Table 1). Although positional differences were not statistically significant, forwards demonstrated a notable trend toward faster RTP (145.5 ± 105.8 days) relative to midfielders (265.0 ± 240.1 days) and defenders (254.2 ± 122.3 days) (P = .149). Player age was significantly associated with recovery duration, as younger athletes (≤26 years) required substantially longer RTP times compared to their older counterparts (303.6 ± 113.0 vs 168.1 ± 99.7 days; P = .017). Weather conditions at the time of injury were not significantly associated with RTP (Table 1).
[FULLTEXT: Return to Play Following Nonruptured Achilles Injuries] RTP following nonruptured Achilles tendon injuries (n = 352) was substantially shorter than for ruptures, with a mean duration of 14.9 ± 65.9 days. RTP trended toward being longer in the later study period (2010-2015: 9.23 ± 28.1 days vs 2016-2021: 19.69 ± 78.4 days), although this did not reach statistical significance (P = .094). Field type, player position, injury setting, and player age were not significantly associated with RTP duration. However, weather conditions at the time of injury significantly influenced recovery, with injuries sustained in sunny, clear, or cloudy conditions resulting in longer RTP durations (14.86 ± 64.82 days) compared to those sustained in rainy or snowy conditions (4.00 ± 10.32 days; P = .019). Additionally, injury mechanism significantly influenced RTP, with players who sustained acute contact injuries returning significantly sooner (4.64 ± 10.68 days) than those with acute noncontact injuries (22.00 ± 50.63 days; P = .031). Chronic or gradual noncontact injuries were associated with intermediate RTP times (15.09 ± 72.01 days), although differences between these and acute contact injuries were not statistically significant (Table 1).
[FULLTEXT: Return to Play and Performance Impacts] The results indicate that Achilles tendon ruptures impose a significant recovery burden, with a mean RTP duration of approximately 229 days. This aligns with prior research in professional athletes, where RTP timelines following Achilles tendon ruptures often range between 6 and 12 months.11,26 However, our data reveal positional disparities in postinjury performance, particularly in forwards and goalkeepers. Forwards who sustained Achilles tendon ruptures exhibited a decline in games played per season the year they were injured. Forwards who had non-ruptured injuries showed a significant decline in games played per season 3 years postinjury, in contrast to matched controls who demonstrated improvements over time. This suggests that Achilles injuries may result in lingering deficits affecting play. 20 These findings are consistent with prior research in elite soccer and basketball players, in whom lower extremity injuries, particularly those affecting tendons, were associated with declines in performance.14,16,26 Goalkeepers demonstrated the most pronounced changes in performance following Achilles ruptures, as evidenced by significant reductions in games played, minutes played, and clean sheets recorded in the first and second years postinjury. Given the biomechanical demands of goalkeeping, requiring frequent explosive vertical and lateral movements, diving saves, and rapid directional changes, the Achilles tendon plays a crucial role in maintaining both agility and responsiveness. The observed decline in postinjury performance reinforces the notion that Achilles injuries may disproportionately affect goalkeepers compared to other positions.2,4 Similar trends have been reported in prior studies examining lower extremity injuries in professional soccer goalkeepers. Hoenig et al 12 noted that goalkeepers most commonly sustained Achilles injuries during explosive starting movements and landing, mechanisms associated with prolonged RTP durations. These findings may underscore the importance of tailored rehabilitation protocols that address the unique biomechanical demands placed on goalkeepers to optimize postinjury recovery and mitigate performance decline.
