[FULLTEXT: Results] At 17.5 ± 6.5 months, DSC achieved the highest healing rate (95.3%) versus PSS (88.9%) and MST (60.6%) (P < 0.001). DSC and PSS were independent predictors of healing (Risk ratios (RR) 12.9 and 4.6 vs. MST). JOA improvement was greatest with DSC (80.53%) and PSS (79.89%) versus MST (67.74%). MST exhibited 12% implant failure (spinous cut-through). No adjacent-disc degeneration was observed in PSS or DSC, supporting short-term motion preservation. CT type II/III defects and overweight status predicted non-union.
[FULLTEXT: Postoperative management and outcome measures] The patients were encouraged to perform axial rolling in bed postoperatively. Drainage tubes were removed after 48 h, while the lumbar brace was worn for 2 months. All patients underwent a standardized follow-up protocol: initial visits at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-surgery, followed by biannual checkups until final evaluation. The timing of temporary instrumentation removal was determined based on radiographic evidence of pars interarticularis healing, with removal occurring between 6 and 12 months postoperatively. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for LBP and the Japanese Orthopaedic Association Lumbar Scoring Scale (JOA-L, 29-point) at baseline, 1 month, 3 months, and the final follow-up [17]. Although JOA-L was originally developed for lumbar spinal stenosis, previous studies have applied it to assess pre- and postoperative symptom changes in patients with lumbar spondylolysis [18, 19]; therefore, we retained this scale in accordance with existing literature.The JOA improvement rate was categorized as excellent (>75%), good (50–75%), fair (25–50%), or ineffective (< 25%) [20]. Radiographic evaluation included standard anteroposterior and lateral lumbar X-rays at each follow-up interval, supplemented by SCT scans at 6 and 12 months and the final visit to assess pars healing and spondylolisthesis reduction. Pars fusion was defined as the presence of continuous trabecular bridging across the defect on CT scans, visible on three or more consecutive sagittal and axial slices. Implant failure was recorded based on breakage, loosening, or migration. MRI assessments were performed postoperatively after implant removal using the Pfirrmann classification to evaluate disc signal changes. The intervertebral discs evaluated on MRI were the adjacent levels to the operated segment (e.g., for L5 spondylolysis, the L4/5 disc was observed; for L4 spondylolysis, the L3/4 and L5/S1 discs were observed). All imaging studies were independently reviewed by two board-certified radiologists with > 5 years of spinal imaging experience; both reviewers were blinded to surgical technique, group allocation, and all clinical outcomes. Any disagreements were adjudicated by a third senior radiologist. Inter-observer agreement for fusion assessment was excellent (Cohen’s κ = 0.902 at 6 months and κ = 0.901 at final follow-up; P < 0.001 for both). In this study, overall treatment success was defined as the simultaneous fulfilment of two criteria: (1) clinical success—postoperative improvement rated as “excellent” or “good” according to the Japanese Orthopaedic Association lumbar (JOA-L) scale; and (2) radiological success—thin-slice CT demonstrating bilateral solid bony union of the pars defect, evidenced by continuous trabecular bridging across the isthmic gap.
[FULLTEXT: Pain and functional outcomes] All three groups showed significant postoperative improvement in VAS and JOA scores compared to baseline (P < 0.001 for all time points). At final follow-up, both the PSS and DSC groups demonstrated significantly lower VAS scores and higher JOA scores than the MST group (P < 0.05), with the DSC group achieving the greatest functional recovery (Table 4). The JOA improvement rate was highest in the DSC group (80.53 ± 11.82%), followed by PSS (79.89 ± 13.51%) and MST (67.74 ± 21.07%) (Table 2,F = 9.571,P < 0.001).
