[FULLTEXT: Results] Cohort and fracture phenotype Of 35,952 public injury records, 329 hand/wrist injuries (0.9%) underwent manual review. The final broad cohort contained 148 fracture events (45.0%) in 142 unique players; this included 63 goalkeeper events (42.6%) and 85 outfield events (57.4%). The subsequent analytic cohorts were overlapping subsets or derived comparison cohorts and therefore should not be summed. Specifically, 98 of the 148 fracture events (66.2%) met the strict explicit metacarpal/phalangeal phenotype; 113 of the 142 players (79.6%) had observable competitive-return follow-up. The matched participation analysis comprised 66 players drawn from the original fracture cohort plus 264 non-fracture same-position controls, yielding 330 matched observations. Thus, the study included 148 validated fracture events overall, not 541 fractures. Median age at the first qualifying fracture was 27.1 years in both groups. These analyses used different, partly overlapping analytic samples rather than separate groups of participants. The strict phenotype included 98 of the 148 fracture events; competitive-return follow-up was available for 113 of 142 players; and the matched participation analysis included 66 fracture cases from the original cohort plus 264 matched controls. These counts refer to different analyses and should not be added together. The study flow and the relationship among the analytic cohorts are shown in Figure 1. The distribution of publicly reported fracture phenotypes differed by playing position (chi-square = 21.48, P < .001; Cramér's V = 0.38). Finger/phalangeal fractures accounted for 31 of 63 goalkeeper events (49.2%) but 15 of 85 outfield events (17.6%), corresponding to a goalkeeper-versus-outfield OR of 4.52 (95% CI, 2.15-9.52; false discovery rate-adjusted q < .001). Metacarpal fractures showed the opposite pattern, accounting for 10 goalkeeper events (15.9%) and 34 outfield events (40.0%) (OR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.13-0.63; q = .004). Thumb and unspecified-hand categories showed no clear independent positional difference (Table 1 and Figure 2). Acute burden and the role of fracture subtype Goalkeepers lost a median of 43 days (interquartile range (IQR), 29-65) and seven matches (IQR, 3.5-11), compared with 18 days (IQR, 11-30) and three matches (IQR, 1-5) among outfield players. From 2012/13 onward, incidence was 18.08 goalkeeper fractures and 2.14 outfield fractures per 1,000 player-seasons, yielding an adjusted incidence rate ratio of 8.38 (95% CI, 5.79-12.12). After adjustment, goalkeepers accumulated 2.10 times the calendar days lost (95% CI, 1.58-2.79), 2.48 times the matches missed (95% CI, 1.82-3.38), and 18.9 additional median days absent (95% CI, 10.7-27.1) (Table 2). The different fracture mix did not explain the goalkeeper time-loss penalty. The position-by-subtype interaction was not significant (P = .407), while the adjusted goalkeeper-to-outfield time-loss ratio remained greater than one in each publicly reported fracture subtype: 2.96 (95% CI, 1.65-5.29) for metacarpal fractures, 1.59 (95% CI, 0.97-2.60) for finger/phalangeal fractures, 2.01 (95% CI, 1.01-4.03) for thumb fractures, and 2.17 (95% CI, 1.38-3.42) for unspecified hand fractures. Thus, the acute positional difference persisted within fracture phenotype rather than being attributable only to a different distribution of labels. Competitive return and two observable time intervals The competitive-return analysis included 113 of 142 players (79.6%) with observable appearance follow-up, comprising 45 of 58 goalkeepers (77.6%) and 68 of 84 outfield players (81.0%). By 28 days after injury onset, five of the 45 goalkeepers (11.1%) and 29 of the 68 outfield players (42.6%) had made a subsequent Big Five appearance. By 60 days, the corresponding numbers were 16 of 45 goalkeepers (35.6%) and 50 of 68 outfield players (73.5%); by 90 days, it was 17 of 45 (37.8%) and 55 of 68 (80.9%), respectively; and by 365 days, 29 of 45 (64.4%) and 64 of 68 (94.1%), respectively. Outfield players returned at nearly three times the instantaneous rate of goalkeepers (hazard ratio (HR), 2.99; 95% CI, 1.83-4.90), and the adjusted odds of a goalkeeper appearing within 90 days were 0.09 (95% CI, 0.03-0.27) relative to an outfield player. The exploratory post-injury-end analysis separated the publicly reported injury episode from subsequent competitive selection. Among 103 fracture episodes in 100 players with an observed appearance after the publicly reported injury-end milestone, public injury duration was longer in goalkeepers (median 35 days, IQR 25.5-47.0) than outfield players (14.5 days, IQR 10.0-30.0). The subsequent lag from the public injury-end milestone to first Big Five appearance had a median of 15 days (IQR 8-176) for goalkeepers and 11 days (IQR 2-40.5) for outfield players (Mann-Whitney U test, P = .023). The more informative difference was in the long tail: 14 of 35 goalkeeper episodes (40.0%) versus 11 of 68 outfield episodes (16.2%) required more than 60 days after the public injury end to reappear, and 14 goalkeeper episodes (40.0%) versus seven outfield episodes (10.3%) required more than 90 days. After adjustment for age, acute time loss, and fracture subtype, goalkeeper episodes had 4.32-fold higher adjusted odds of a post-end lag greater than 60 days (95% CI, 1.47-12.69; q = .012) and 6.94-fold higher adjusted odds of a lag greater than 90 days (95% CI, 2.06-23.41; q = .005) (Table 3 and Figure 3). Matched cohort analysis The primary matched cohort comprised 330 observations: 66 fracture cases (20.0%) and 264 same-position controls (80.0%). Among the fracture cases, 18 were goalkeepers (27.3%) and 48 were outfield players (72.7%); among controls, 72 were goalkeepers (27.3%) and 192 were outfield players (72.7%). Balance was close for age and baseline minutes.
